Efficiency

  • What is efficiency?
  • Efficient $\ne$ good
  • Pareto: If everyone agrees outcome $A$ is better for them personally than outcome $B$, then $B$ is not efficient
  • So (?): if not everyone agrees there is a (feasible) better outcome than $C$, then $C$ is efficient
  • In other words: if $C$ is efficient and $D$ is possible, some may prefer $D$, but not everyone
  • E.g. We divide 100 apples
  • Is it efficient to share them equally?
  • Is giving them all to me efficient?
  • What would be inefficient?

Why Pareto Efficiency?

  • Advantages:
    1. Agnostic: uses judgement of agents, not economsists
    2. Conservative: if outcome is not efficient, everyone agrees it is bad
  • Disadvantages:
    1. Conservative: if outcome is efficient, not everyone agrees it is good
    2. Favours status quo
    3. In a given situation, that means it values the rich more

General Equilibrium

  • Many markets at the same time, one for each good ($A$, $B$, $C$ etc.)
  • Consumers and producers see prices and choose demand and supply
  • (Choices for everyone together are called outcomes or allocations)
  • In a market, if Demand=Supply for given prices, we say that market clears
  • Equilibrium: prices that clear all markets simultaneously
  • Difficulty (mathematically): price of good A affects demand for good B
  • Does equilibrium even exist?
  • 1954: Arrow and Debreu show yes.

Welfare Theorem(s)

1st welfare theorem: Very strong assumptions $\Rightarrow$ market outcome Pareto Efficient
  • Price taking
    • Agents cannot influence the price
    • Perfect competition
  • Complete markets
    • Everything that matters has a market and a price
    • No externalities (pollution, knowledge spillover)
    • No asymmetric information (good and bad cars selling in the same market)

Proof of 1st Welfare Theorem

  • For simplicity, we'll assume production is fixed, total $Q_A$ of good $A$, total $Q_B$ of good $B$ etc.
  • Market A clears $\Rightarrow$ total amount of good A is demanded: $D_A=Q_A$
  • Then total cost of market $A$ is $Q_Ap_A$
  • In equilibrium all markets clear $\Rightarrow$ total equilibrium cost $TC^*=Q_A p_A+Q_B p_B+Q_Cp_C+\ldots$
  • Other feasible outcomes: no more than $Q_A$ of good $A$, no more than $Q_B$ of good $B$ etc.
  • So cost of market $A$ (at equilibrium prices) is less than equilibrium cost of market $A$
  • So: feasible only if $TC\le TC^*$

Proof of 1st Welfare Theorem ctd.

  • If an outcome is feasible, its total cost $TC$ (at equilibrium prices) is less than $TC^*$
  • Now consider outcomes everyone likes better (or at least not less)
  • For every consumer that outcome must cost more than their income (or at least not less)
  • Otherwise they would have just bought the other outcome instead
  • But then the total cost for all consumers of this outcome must be greater than the the total cost of equilibrium
  • feasible: $TC\le TC^*$, improvement for everyone: $TC>TC^*$
  • There are no feasible outcomes that improve things for everybody
  • Equilibrium is efficient
  • Can you spot where we used the two assumptions?
  • Can you spot the hidden third (technical) assumption?

Why is Equilibrium Efficient? Adam Smith

It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest.

An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, Book 1, Chapter 2.

Every individual endeavours to employ his capital so that its produce may be of the greatest value. He generally neither intends to promote the public interest, nor knows how much he is promoting it. He intends only his own security, only his own gain. And he is in this led by an invisible hand to promote an end which was no part of his intention. By pursuing his own interest he frequently promotes that of the society more effectually than he really intends to promote it.

Why is Equilibrium Efficient? Friedrich Hayek

We must look at the price system as (...) a mechanism for communicating information if we want to understand its real function (...) The most significant fact about this system is the economy of knowledge with which it operates, or how little the individual participants need to know in order to be able to take the right action (...) by a kind of symbol, only the most essential information is passed on (...)

The Use of Knowledge in Society (as quoted by Grossman)

Fundamentally, in a system in which the knowledge of the relevant facts is dispersed among many people, prices can act to coordinate the separate actions of different people (...)

Ibid.

Their point

  • Markets organize production and allocation without any individual knowing or understanding the whole economy.
  • Prices pass along all relevant information. Increasing price means more of this good is needed.
  • Individuals respond in a self-interested way to prices and yet the resulting production and allocation decision are optimal in some way.

Long-run Equilibrium

  • If profits in a market are greater than in other markets ⇒ new firms come in
    • Assumption: No barriers to entry
  • Drives down profits
  • In the long run profits go to zero
  • From price taking, we know $P=MC$
  • 0-profits ⇒ Revenue=Cost
  • $PQ=C$ ⇒ $P=\dfrac CQ=AC$
  • So $MC=P=AC$

Market Failures

  • Two assumptions: price-taking and complete markets
  • If they fail (they always do) ⇒ no efficiency
  • No price-taking ⇒ market power:
    • Producers or consumers can affect prices
    • Monopoly (Monopsony)
    • Oligopoly
  • No complete markets:
    • Externalities - Positive (knowledge spillover) or Negative (pollution)
    • Asymmetric information
      • Adverse Selection
      • Moral Hazard

Sandwiches Revisited: a Monopolist

  • Baptist was price taker: for given $P$, decided how many sandwiches to sell
  • In equilibrium $P=MC$, efficient
  • What if he realised his market power?
  • If he is the only sandwich seller, he could set any price and see who buys
  • (Reminder: each sandwich costs €1 to make, 180 potential customers: 60 would pay €3, 40 would pay €2, 80 would pay €.50)
  • If he sets $P=2$, 100 people buy: Profit$=(P-C)Q=(2-1)\cdot 100=100$
  • If he sets $P=3$, 100 people buy: Profit$=(P-C)Q=(3-1)\cdot 600=120$
  • Best price: $P=3$

Sandwiches Revisited: a Monopolist

  • $P=3>1=MC$
  • Not efficient. Why?
  • Baptist could sell to 40 more people, for € 1.5, and they'd both benefit
  • He can't do that because others would complain (single price)
  • Not efficient, but Baptist is pretty happy
  • Monopolist keeps production low, so he can keep prices high. Hurts consumers
  • Lerner index: how much too high is the price
  • $L=\dfrac{P-MC}P=\dfrac{3-1}3=66\%$

Monopoly

  • For any firm: $\pi=R(q)-C(q)$
  • FOC: $R'(Q)-C'(Q)=0$ ⇒ $MR=MC$
  • For price taker, revenue: $R=P\cdot Q$ ⇒ $MR=P$
  • For monopolist, revenue $R=P(Q)\cdot Q$ ⇒ $MR=P{\color{red}+Q\cdot P'}$
  • MR is always lower for monopolist ⇒ produces less
  • Monopolist produces less to keep price high: $P=MC-Q\cdot P'>MC$

Measures of competition

  • Monopolist: alone in market
  • If more players, still some market power
  • Lerner index is one measure of lack of competition
  • Herfindahl-Hirschmann index: measure of market concentration
  • If $m_i$ is the market share (in sales, for example) of firm $i$
  • $H=m_1^2+m_2^2+\ldots+m_n^2=\sum_{i=0}^n m_i^2$
  • If one big and many small firms: $H=.99^2+.001^2+\ldots .0001^2\approx 99\%$
  • If $n$ equal sized firms: $H= (\dfrac 1n)^2+(\dfrac 1n)^2+\ldots =n\cdot(\dfrac 1n)^2=\dfrac 1n$
  • If $n\rightarrow\infty$, $H\rightarrow 0$
